Smart Health

The Brief

Healthcare is broken. Let's fix the check-in.

The lack of a central platform for storing medical records and facilitating communication between patients and caregivers leads to fragmented care, frustrated patients, and missed opportunities for collaboration. An application was needed to empower United States patients to manage their health information and securely connect with caregivers across the US healthcare system.

The needs were clear: secure data storage, a user-friendly interface, and controlled information sharing that worked for both patients and their US-based care teams.

Process

Five steps. One goal, simplify healthcare.

The process ran through five structured steps, condensed into four phases. Working across time zones with a US-based client added complexity, but each phase had a clear focus and output that kept the team aligned.

01
Understand
US Healthcare Research
Started by deeply studying the US healthcare system, its fragmented structure, insurance models, and the daily friction patients face. Sessions with a Subject Matter Expert and PM helped translate complex healthcare workflows into concrete design requirements.
Target Users
Alex Turner
35 years
Challenges
Keeping track of multiple medical appointments, understanding complex medical jargon, finding time for regular check-ups.
Design decision
The Home tab anchors on the next appointment with QR check-in as a single tap. Health records use plain-language summaries throughout, no clinical labels.
Maria Rodriguez
62 years
Challenges
Managing complex medication schedules, scheduling appointments, understanding medical reports, feeling overwhelmed by caregiving responsibilities.
Design decision
Medications use a timeline view showing the daily regimen at a glance rather than a flat list. Medical reports surface a plain-English summary before the clinical detail.
02
Define
Competitive Benchmarking
Reviewed existing insurance and healthcare apps available in the USA to understand established patterns and user expectations.
  • United Healthcare, complex but feature-rich, high trust signals
  • CVS, pharmacy-first approach, clear medication tracking
  • Cigna Healthcare, insurance-centric, dense information architecture
From United Healthcare, we adopted prominent verification status and explicit privacy signals in the check-in flow, trust that is visible rather than assumed. From Cigna, one thing worked despite the density, the insurance card was always one tap away. That became a persistent bottom tab. What all three shared, and what we rejected, was the app-as-portal framing, dense nested navigation, and insurance-first hierarchy that puts the wrong thing front and centre for a patient who just needs to check in.
Constraint
Limited familiarity with industry-specific regulations, terminologies, and workflows, coupled with the need to coordinate effectively across different time zones, impacted project timelines and communication efficiency.
03
Design
Defined the app structure around a five-tab bottom navigation: Home, Profile, Insurance, Network, and Inbox. The IA was designed to give patients a single source of truth for their health identity, connecting QR check-in, insurance cards, health summary, and care network in one place.
Five tabs because each maps to a mental model patients already hold, my visit, my records, my coverage, my care team, my messages. A hamburger menu would have buried Insurance and Network, the two highest-anxiety touch points for users like Alex and Maria.
Ran rapid design and prototyping sessions with PM and Tech to validate flows early. The verification flow, covering MFA, government ID upload, and ID.me integration, was a key focus given the security requirements of US healthcare data.
04
Deliver
UX & UI Audit + Handover
Before handover, ran a full audit across every screen, checking component states (default, active, error, empty), edge case flows, iOS Human Interface Guideline compliance, and style system consistency throughout. The audit was the final quality gate, the point where anything that had been deferred during sprint execution either got resolved or flagged with a clear note for development. What was handed over included annotated Figma specs, flow documentation for all key user journeys, and explicit dev notes for the verification flow covering MFA, government ID upload, and ID.me integration, given the security complexity of that path.
Outcome

A complete MVP. Delivered on brief.

The MVP brought together a patient's full health identity, insurance, records, care network, and inbox, into a single app experience. The design made healthcare check-in as simple as showing a QR code, eliminating repeated paperwork and reducing friction for both patients and providers.

Centralized Medical Records
A platform to consolidate patient health information for easy access and reference, from allergies and conditions to medications.
Secure Communication
A secure channel for patients and caregivers to exchange information and updates, with identity-verified access.
Patient Empowerment
Tools to help patients understand and manage their health, with an Information Zone for proactive health content.
Improved Collaboration
Seamless communication and information sharing among healthcare providers, with primary care and network connections built in.
Delivered. Fully designed, audited, and handed over.
A client-side decision delayed the launch. The design was complete.
Learnings

What this project taught me

Domain knowledge is a design tool
US healthcare has its own vocabulary, workflows, and trust expectations. Investing time upfront with the SME meant fewer wrong turns and more confident design decisions throughout.
Cross-timezone collaboration needs structure
Working with a US-based client from India meant every sync had to be purposeful. Tight documentation and async-first communication kept the project moving without waiting for overlapping hours.
Trust is designed in layers
Healthcare users need to trust the app before they hand over sensitive health data. The verification flow, clear privacy controls, and ID.me integration weren't just features, they were the foundation of the entire experience.
Simplicity wins in high-stakes contexts
The temptation was to pack in features. The right call was restraint, a clean home screen that surfaces the most important information, and nothing more. The QR check-in concept worked precisely because it was one step, not five.
